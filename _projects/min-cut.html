Ollivier–Ricci curvature as a minimum cut

Problem. To compute the exact Ollivier–Ricci curvature of an edge without a distance matrix or a linear program.

Ollivier–Ricci curvature compares the random walks at the two ends of an edge by the cost of moving one onto the other, κ = 1 − W1, and that cost is usually a linear program over a matrix of graph distances. When both endpoints have degree d, the matrix has (d + 1)2 entries, and solving the program exactly takes O(d3 log d) time. In Proposition I, I show that for any one-step random walk, the dual of that program has an optimum with three levels, so the curvature of an edge is one minimum cut on a network built from adjacency lists alone.

The curvature is due to Ollivier (2009); W1 is the Wasserstein distance for the shortest-path metric.

The O(d3 log d) bound is Orlin’s (1993), for transportation problems.

What you are seeing

The upper figure is the graph, with the chosen edge drawn thick. The walks from its two ends spread their mass over the ends’ neighbours. Where both put mass, the common part stays put, and the rest has to move from the red vertices to the blue ones, each drawn in proportion to its share. The lines are an optimal plan for that move: solid between neighbours, dashed between vertices two steps apart, dotted between vertices three apart.

The same cost can be found without a plan, by numbering the vertices. Give each red vertex a potential of 1, 2 or 3 and each blue vertex one of 0, 1 or 2, so that no red vertex sits more than its distance above any blue one. The numbering that makes the red masses times their potentials, minus the blue masses times theirs, as large as possible gives exactly the cost of the best plan. Those are the numbers inside the vertices.

The lower figure finds that numbering with one cut. Each vertex of the upper figure has two nodes in it, one in each band: a red vertex’s nodes ask whether its potential is at least 3 and at least 2, a blue vertex’s whether it is at least 2 and at least 1. An arc means that one yes forces another, so that no pair breaks the distance rule: an adjacent pair needs two arcs, a pair two steps apart one, a pair three apart none. The lines from s and to t carry the masses, a yes earning a red vertex’s mass and paying a blue one’s, and a minimum cut picks the answers worth the most. Its yes answers are the filled nodes, its arcs are the yellow ones, and its capacity F gives the cost, W1 = 3A − F, where A is the mass that moves.

Rows are sorted by potential, so the yes answers gather at the top of each band.

How it is computed

Everything runs in your browser from the graph’s adjacency lists. The maximum flow is Dinic’s algorithm on integer masses, so every number is an exact fraction.

The karate club is Zachary’s, 34 members and 78 friendships. The random geometric graph is the one on the screening map, the largest component of 800 random points in the unit square at expected degree 8, shown at a fixed scale around the chosen edge.
