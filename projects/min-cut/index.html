Ollivier–Ricci curvature as a minimum cut

Problem. To compute the exact Ollivier–Ricci curvature of an edge without a distance matrix or a linear program.

Ollivier–Ricci curvature compares the random walks at the two ends of an edge by the cost of moving one onto the other, κ = 1 − W1, and that cost is usually a linear program over a matrix of graph distances. In Proposition I, I show that for any walk that stays within one step, the dual of that program has an optimum with three levels, so the curvature of an edge is one minimum cut on a network built from adjacency lists alone.

The curvature is due to Ollivier (2009); W1 is the Wasserstein distance for the shortest-path metric.

Click an edge of the graph and the page builds that edge’s network, cuts it, and reads off both the curvature and the certificate that it is optimal.

What you are seeing

The upper figure is the graph, with the chosen edge in black. The walks at its two ends put mass on their neighbourhoods; mass common to both stays where it is, and the rest has to move from the red vertices, drawn in proportion to what they send, to the blue ones. The lines between them are an optimal transport plan.

The lower figure is the network of the paper. Each red vertex has two nodes, one for each step its potential can rise, and so does each blue vertex. Arcs between them encode the constraints of the dual, ux − vy ≤ dist(x, y): two arcs for an adjacent pair, one for a pair with a common neighbour, and none for a pair at distance 3. The filled nodes are the source side of a minimum cut and the yellow arcs are the cut, whose capacity is the maximum flow F; the curvature follows from W1 = 3A − F, where A is the mass that moves.

The rows are sorted by potential, so the source side gathers at the top of each band.

The cut is also a proof. A red vertex’s potential is 1 plus the number of its filled nodes, a blue vertex’s the number of its filled nodes, and these potentials satisfy every constraint of the dual. The plan is a second, smaller flow along the pairs where a constraint is tight, and it costs exactly 3A − F, so no other plan can cost less.

How it is computed

Everything runs in your browser from the two endpoints’ adjacency lists. A pair is at distance 1 if it is adjacent, 2 if it has a common neighbour, and 3 otherwise, since the edge itself joins any two of these vertices in three steps; no distance matrix is built and no linear program is solved. The maximum flow is Dinic’s algorithm on integer masses, so every number is an exact fraction.

The page draws the plain network. The paper’s solver, orcut, also routes blocks of distance-2 pairs through relay nodes, merges vertices with the same neighbours and starts from a warm flow; these change the network but not the value. On both graphs here, every lazy value equals orcut’s, and every value for the three measures agrees with a linear program on breadth-first distances.

The solver on its own is the Python package faster-or-curvature.
